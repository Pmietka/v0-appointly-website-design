# How Do Concrete Coating Companies Keep Their Crews Busy in Winter?

**Meta Description:** Cold market coating crews stay busy in winter with indoor floors, cold rated systems used within spec, spring jobs pre-sold with deposits, and a cash plan for crew.

---

Concrete coating companies in cold markets keep crews busy in winter by moving to floors where the slab stays warm (basements, heated garages, commercial and shop floors), installing cold rated systems only within the manufacturer's spec sheet, and pre-selling spring installs with deposits. The fourth piece is cash: set aside money in season so your best installers are still on payroll in March. Florida, the Gulf Coast, and the Carolinas book year round, so this plan is for everyone else.

## Key takeaways

- In most of the country, floor coating is strong spring through fall and drops in winter. Florida, the Gulf Coast, and the Carolinas are the exception.
- Winter work comes from floors that stay warm: basements, heated garages and shops, and commercial interiors.
- Many epoxy systems want the slab comfortably above 50°F, and many polyaspartic and polyurea systems are rated to install colder. The spec sheet is the rule, not the weather app.
- Pre-selling spring installs with a deposit turns winter estimates into a full April and cash in hand.
- The crew you keep through winter is the crew that installs your spring backlog. Losing a trained installer in January costs more than carrying one.
- Winter is an operations and cash problem first. The ad plan is a separate question.

## What indoor work can a coating crew do in winter?

The rule is simple: if the slab and the space stay inside your system's temperature range, you can install. That means looking for floors inside heated or conditioned buildings.

| Winter work | Winter demand | Skill and equipment | Margin notes | How to find it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basements | Steady from homeowners finishing or cleaning out basements | Moisture testing is critical; ventilation and odor matter in an occupied home | Usually smaller square footage; price a minimum job | Past garage customers, basement finishing contractors |
| Heated residential garages and shops | Present in every market, smaller pool | Same as a normal garage install; confirm the slab, not just the air, is in range | Normal garage pricing holds | Ask every fall lead if the garage is heated |
| Commercial and shop floors | Many businesses prefer slow season installs | Larger grinders, night or weekend work, insurance certificates, written bids | Different pricing model than residential; bid carefully | Auto shops, showrooms, warehouses, gyms you already drive past |
| Recoats and repairs on interior floors | Small but recurring | Topcoat compatibility, chip and crack repair | Small tickets; good filler between bigger jobs | Your own past customers |
| Spring prep visits | High for homeowners who booked spring | Measuring, moisture testing, photos, color selection | No install revenue, but locks the job | Every winter estimate and every deposit |

Commercial work is the biggest lever and the biggest change in how you bid and get paid. ## Can you install garage coatings in winter with a cold rated system?

Sometimes, and only inside the spec sheet. Many epoxy systems want the slab comfortably above 50°F. Many polyaspartic and polyurea systems are rated to install colder, which is why northern contractors lean on them. The manufacturer's spec sheet for your exact product is the rule. Our [polyaspartic vs epoxy](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors) breakdown covers the tradeoffs between the systems.

Before you sell a winter garage install, run this checklist:

1. **Read the spec sheet.** Write down the minimum slab temperature, the minimum air temperature, and any dew point rule. Keep a copy in the truck.
2. **Measure the slab, not the air.** A garage can feel fine while the concrete is far colder. Use an infrared thermometer on the slab, in several spots, before you grind.
3. **Log it.** Record slab and air temperature on every winter job. If a floor ever fails, that log is your answer.
4. **Only heat the space if the manufacturer allows it.** Some heaters add moisture or fumes. Follow the spec sheet, not a forum post.
5. **Match the warranty to the conditions.** Do not warranty an install you did outside the spec. If the conditions are not there, reschedule.

If the system cannot handle the conditions, the honest answer is a spring date with a deposit.

## How do you pre-sell spring installs with deposits?

Every winter estimate is a spring job you can lock in now.

- **Run the estimate in winter.** Measure, test moisture, pick the color, and show the system.
- **Give a specific install window.** "The second week of April" is easier to say yes to than "sometime in spring."
- **Take a deposit to hold the date.** Put the amount, the refund terms, and what happens if weather pushes the date in writing. Check whether your state limits deposits on home improvement contracts.
- **Lock the price, with an end date.** A price that holds for a spring install is a reason to sign now, not a discount.
- **Do not spend the deposit as profit.** Treat it as material money for that job. A deposit spent in January on payroll is a hole in April.
- **Keep them warm.** A booked spring install that sits for weeks needs a confirmation and reminders, just like any estimate.

> **From the field:** Estimates booked far ahead are the ones most likely to slip. Across accounts, about 80% of booked estimates show with no reminders, and about 95% show when the homeowner is confirmed at booking, reminded 24 hours and 1 hour out, and followed up when they go quiet. ## How do you handle cash flow from December to March?

Winter cash is planned in summer. A few habits keep the slow months from turning into a crisis:

- **Set aside money every job in season.** Pick a fixed share of every paid invoice and move it to a separate account. The amount depends on your overhead, so do the math on your own fixed costs per winter month.
- **Know your winter burn.** Add up rent, insurance, truck payments, software, and the payroll you plan to keep. That is the number your savings and winter jobs need to cover.
- **Arrange credit before you need it.** A line of credit is easier to get in July with a full calendar than in January with an empty one.
- **Service equipment while it is cheap to be down.** It also gives the crew paid work.
- **Watch material shelf life.** Do not stock up on product that will expire before spring.

## How do you keep your best installers through winter?

Your spring backlog is only worth something if a trained crew is there to install it. Our guide to [hiring and training a floor coating crew](/blog/how-to-hire-and-train-a-floor-coating-crew) covers what that takes.

| Option | What it looks like | Good for | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full payroll | Crew stays on full hours doing indoor jobs, prep visits, and shop work | Owners with commercial or basement work lined up | Costs the most; needs real work to fill the days |
| Reduced hours | Guaranteed minimum hours, more when jobs come in | Owners with some winter work and some savings | Installers may pick up other work |
| Shop and training days | Paid days for equipment service, training, and practice boards | Every cold market company, a few days a month | Pure cost, no install revenue |
| Seasonal layoff with a rehire date | Crew leaves for winter with a written spring start date | Owners with no winter work and thin cash | Your best people may not come back |

Most cold market owners mix these: keep the crew lead on full or reduced hours, put everyone on a few paid shop days, and staff indoor jobs as they come.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can concrete coating companies work in winter?

Yes, in heated or conditioned spaces. Basements, heated garages and shops, and commercial interiors keep the slab inside most systems' temperature range. Concrete coating crews in cold markets also install cold rated systems in garages, but only within the manufacturer's spec sheet for slab and air temperature.

### What is the coldest temperature for installing a garage floor coating?

It depends on the product. Many epoxy systems want the slab comfortably above 50°F, and many polyaspartic and polyurea systems are rated to install colder. The manufacturer's spec sheet for your exact garage floor coating system is the rule. Measure the slab, not the air.

### Should epoxy flooring companies lay off crews in winter?

Only as a last resort. A trained epoxy installer is hard to replace before spring, and the crew you keep is the crew that installs your spring backlog. Most owners mix reduced hours, paid shop and training days, and indoor jobs to keep their best people through winter.

### Do floor coating companies in Florida have a slow winter?

Florida, the Gulf Coast, and the Carolinas book year round, so the cold market winter plan does not apply. The rest of the country is strong spring through fall and drops in winter, which is when indoor work and spring deposits matter most.
