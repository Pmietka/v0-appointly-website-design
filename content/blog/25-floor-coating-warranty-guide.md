# Floor Coating Warranty Guide: What to Cover, What to Exclude, and How to Use It to Close

**Meta Description:** What a garage floor coating warranty should cover, what it must exclude, how long to offer, and how to use it to close estimates without eating callbacks.

---

## The Warranty Is a Sales Tool and a Liability Document at the Same Time

Every homeowner sitting across from you at the kitchen table has read a horror story about a peeling garage floor. Your warranty is the answer to the question they are not asking out loud: "What happens if this fails?" A clear, confident warranty closes jobs. A vague one costs you callbacks, refunds, and one-star reviews.

This guide covers what a residential floor coating warranty should say, what it must exclude, how long to offer, and how to present it during the estimate.

## What Actually Fails, and Why

Before writing a warranty, be honest about what goes wrong with coated floors:

- **Delamination and peeling.** Almost always a prep failure: the slab was not ground to the right profile, a sealer or old coating was left behind, or the base coat was applied over dust or oil.
- **Moisture blistering.** Vapor pushing up through the slab lifts the coating in bubbles. The cause is a slab that needed a moisture test and a vapor-mitigating primer.
- **Hot tire pickup.** Cheaper epoxies soften under a hot tire and pull up in a tire-shaped patch. Polyaspartic topcoats largely eliminate this, which is one reason to sell [polyaspartic over epoxy](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors).
- **Yellowing.** Epoxy ambers in sunlight. Not a failure, but homeowners treat it like one.
- **Cracks telegraphing through.** Concrete moves. A coating bridges hairline cracks, not structural ones.

Notice that the first two are on you, the third is a product choice, and the last two are the concrete's fault. Your warranty should reflect exactly that.

## What to Cover

Keep the covered list short and specific. The standard residential coating warranty covers:

1. **Adhesion.** The coating will not peel, flake, or delaminate from a properly prepared slab.
2. **Workmanship.** Application defects such as roller marks, holidays, uneven flake, or missed edges will be corrected.
3. **Product defects.** Pass through the manufacturer's warranty for the materials themselves.

That is it. Everything else is either an exclusion or a maintenance item.

## What to Exclude

This is where most contractors get burned. Write these exclusions plainly and initial them with the homeowner:

- **Moisture vapor transmission** on slabs that were not moisture tested, or that tested above the manufacturer's limit and the homeowner declined vapor mitigation.
- **Structural cracking, heaving, or settling** of the concrete.
- **Damage from impact, gouging, dragging equipment, or chemical spills** beyond the product's rating.
- **Discoloration from UV exposure** on epoxy systems. Say this in writing every time you install epoxy.
- **Hot tire pickup on epoxy-only systems**, unless you are selling a polyaspartic topcoat.
- **Normal wear**, including gloss loss in traffic lanes.
- **Any coating applied over an existing coating** at the homeowner's request.
- **Work by others**, including anyone who repairs or recoats the floor after you.

The moisture exclusion only holds up if you actually test. Buy a moisture meter, test every slab, and write the reading on the estimate. A number on paper ends most arguments before they start.

## How Long to Offer

The industry ranges from one year to "lifetime." Here is what the numbers actually mean:

- **One to two years** signals you are not confident. Homeowners notice.
- **Five to ten years** is the sweet spot for a full flake polyaspartic system with proper prep. Long enough to feel serious, short enough that you can honor it.
- **Fifteen years to lifetime** is a marketing device. If you offer it, make it pro-rated, non-transferable, and limited to adhesion on a slab you tested. Read the fine print on the "lifetime" warranties your franchise competitors advertise and you will find exactly that.

For a new coating business, a straightforward ten-year adhesion and workmanship warranty is easy to explain and easy to honor. Your material supplier's warranty on the product itself is separate. Understand it before you promise anything.

## Make the Warranty Conditional on the Right Things

Attach conditions that protect the floor and cost the homeowner nothing:

- **Cure time.** No foot traffic for the stated hours and no vehicles until the product's full cure. Write the exact hours on the invoice.
- **Cleaning.** pH-neutral cleaners only. No harsh solvents, no pressure washing at close range.
- **Mats under hot tires** are optional on polyaspartic and required on epoxy.
- **Report defects within 30 days** of noticing them.

Conditions like these do not scare homeowners. They signal that you know the product.

## How to Present It at the Estimate

Do not hand over the warranty at the end as fine print. Use it in the middle of the pitch, right after you explain prep.

> Here's why we grind instead of acid etch, and why we test the slab for moisture before we start. Your floor comes with a ten-year warranty on adhesion and workmanship, and I can only offer that because of the prep. The companies that skip the grind can't warranty the floor, and that's usually why their quote is cheaper.

You have just turned your warranty into the reason your price is higher. This is the core of selling on value in our guide to [floor coating pricing and margins](/blog/floor-coating-pricing-and-margins), and it works especially well when the homeowner is comparing you to a lower quote.

## Handling a Warranty Claim Without Losing the Customer

Callbacks will happen. Handle them like this and they turn into reviews instead of disputes:

1. **Respond the same day.** Even if you cannot get there for a week, the homeowner needs to know you are not dodging.
2. **Go look before you decide.** Most claims are smaller than they sound on the phone.
3. **Fix covered items without an argument.** A half-day repair is cheaper than a one-star review, and a repaired floor with a happy homeowner produces the [Google review](/blog/google-reviews-floor-coating-contractors) that closes your next three jobs.
4. **Explain excluded items with the paperwork.** Show the moisture reading, the initialed exclusion, the cure-time instructions. Offer a paid repair at a fair price.

Budget for it. One to two percent of revenue set aside for warranty work covers a well-run coating business.

## A Simple Warranty Template Outline

Your attorney should review the final document, but the structure is standard:

- Company name, customer name, address, install date, system installed
- Coverage: adhesion and workmanship, for a stated term, non-transferable unless you choose otherwise
- Exclusions: the list above
- Conditions: cure, cleaning, reporting
- Remedy: repair or recoat the affected area at the company's discretion; no cash refunds
- Moisture test result and prep method recorded on the document
- Homeowner signature and initials beside the exclusions

Keep a signed copy with the job photos. Together they are your complete defense on any dispute and your complete proof for the next homeowner who asks "what happens if it fails?"
