# Floor Coating Business Insurance: The Policies Epoxy Contractors Actually Need

**Meta Description:** Floor coating business insurance explained: general liability, completed operations, auto, tools, workers comp, pollution, umbrella, and what goes on a COI.

---

Floor coating business insurance usually starts with general liability that includes completed operations, commercial auto for the truck and trailer, inland marine coverage for the grinder and tools, and workers' compensation once you have employees, where your state requires it. Many coating contractors add contractors pollution liability and an umbrella as they grow or take commercial work. What it costs depends on your state, payroll, revenue, and claims history, so the only real number is the one on a quote.

**Not legal or insurance advice.** This is a plain language overview for planning. Coverage depends on the exact policy wording, and rules differ by state. Talk to a licensed commercial insurance agent, and to your state's agencies, before you rely on any of it.

## Key takeaways

- General liability with completed operations coverage is the foundation. It responds to injury and property damage claims from your work, including after the job is finished.
- General liability is generally not a warranty fund. Redoing your own peeling floor is usually a business cost, not a covered claim.
- Personal auto policies may not cover a truck and trailer used for business. Ask your agent before your first job.
- Your grinder and vac are worth thousands and live in a trailer. Inland marine or equipment coverage protects them on the road and on site.
- Workers' comp rules vary by state. California requires it even with one employee. Texas lets most private employers choose.
- GCs, property managers, and some homeowners will ask for a certificate of insurance, and commercial clients often want to be named as additional insured.

## What insurance does a floor coating business need?

Here are the core coverages most coating contractors look at, in rough order of when they come up.

| Coverage | What it is for in a coating business | When you need it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General liability | Third party bodily injury and property damage from your operations, such as a homeowner tripping over a cord or overspray on a car | Before your first job |
| Products and completed operations | Claims that arise after the job is done, such as an injury tied to the finished floor | Before your first job. Confirm it is included in your GL |
| Commercial auto | The truck, van, and trailer you use for work | Before you drive to a job with the business vehicle |
| Inland marine (contractor's equipment or tools) | Grinder, vac, edge grinder, and tools stolen or damaged in transit or on site | As soon as you own equipment you cannot afford to replace |
| Workers' compensation | Medical care and lost wages for employees hurt on the job | When you hire, or earlier if your state or clients require it |
| Contractors pollution liability | Claims from fumes, spills, or releases of coatings, solvents, and cleaning products | Worth a conversation early, often required for commercial work |
| Umbrella or excess liability | Extra limits on top of GL, auto, and employer's liability | When clients ask for higher limits, or your exposure grows |

The SBA's guide to [getting business insurance](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/get-business-insurance) lays out the same basic logic: buy what the law requires first, then insure against the things you could not afford to pay for on your own, and use a licensed agent to compare terms and prices. It also notes that businesses with employees may be legally required to carry coverage like workers' compensation and unemployment insurance, and that the requirements vary by state.

## What does general liability cover for a floor coating contractor?

General liability is the policy homeowners and GCs ask about first. For a coating crew, think of the claims it is designed for: the homeowner's kid slips on a wet base coat, your crew backs the trailer into the neighbor's mailbox (that one may fall under auto instead), flake ends up all over a car parked in the garage, or a cord across the driveway trips a delivery driver.

Two things to get clear with your agent:

1. **Completed operations.** Coating claims often show up after you leave. Make sure your GL includes products and completed operations coverage and ask what the completed operations aggregate is. GCs often ask for this specifically.
2. **Your own work.** Liability policies generally do not pay to fix or replace your own faulty work. If a floor delaminates because the slab was not ground right, the cost to redo it usually comes out of your pocket. That is what the reserve in our [floor coating warranty guide](/blog/floor-coating-warranty-guide) is for. Damage that your failed work causes to other property can be a different story, so ask how your policy treats it.

Limits of $1 million per occurrence are common for residential work, and commercial clients often ask for $2 million or more, as our guide to [commercial floor coating jobs](/blog/commercial-floor-coating-jobs-for-residential-contractors) notes. Your contracts decide what you need, not a rule of thumb.

Describe your work accurately when you apply. "Flooring" is not the same as "grinding concrete and applying epoxy and polyaspartic coatings in occupied homes." The wrong classification can mean the wrong price, or a policy that does not fit the work you do.

## Do you need commercial auto and equipment coverage?

**Commercial auto.** Personal auto policies may limit or exclude coverage for vehicles used mainly for business. If the truck is titled to the business, regularly hauls a trailer full of equipment, or is driven by employees, talk to your agent about commercial auto and confirm the trailer is covered for both liability and physical damage.

**Inland marine for tools and equipment.** A walk-behind grinder and dust extractor can easily represent $6,000 to $15,000 of your startup budget, as our [floor coating equipment list](/blog/floor-coating-equipment-list) shows. That equipment moves between job sites and sits in a trailer overnight. A contractor's equipment or tools floater (part of inland marine coverage) is the usual way to insure it. Ask:

- Is coverage on a scheduled list (each item and value listed) or a blanket limit?
- Is it replacement cost or actual cash value?
- Does it cover theft from a locked trailer, and does it require the trailer to be locked or alarmed?
- What is the deductible, and is the policy worth it for smaller tools?

Keep serial numbers and photos of every machine. It makes claims and police reports much faster.

## What are the workers' comp rules for coating contractors?

Workers' compensation is set by state law, and the rules differ more than most new owners expect. Two examples from the states themselves:

- **California.** The state's Division of Workers' Compensation [says employers are required by law to carry workers' compensation insurance, even if they have only one employee](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dwc/Employer.htm).
- **Texas.** The Texas Department of Insurance [says private employers can choose to carry workers' compensation coverage, but it is not required in most cases](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/wc/employer/index.html). Employers without it, called non-subscribers, must report that to the state and report certain injuries.

Most states fall somewhere between, with rules about how many employees trigger the requirement, whether owners and LLC members can exclude themselves, and how subcontractors are treated. A few points apply almost everywhere:

- **Uninsured subs can land on your policy.** If you pay a helper as a 1099 subcontractor and they have no coverage of their own, many carriers will charge for them at the annual audit. Collect certificates from every sub.
- **Payroll drives the premium.** Comp is generally priced on payroll and the classification of the work, adjusted by claims history. Expect an audit.
- **Clients may require it even when your state does not.** GCs and commercial property managers often ask for a comp certificate, or a waiver of subrogation, before you set foot on site.

A safe shop helps here too. Silica controls, respirators, and good mixing practices reduce the injuries that drive comp costs. If you are about to hire, read our guide to [hiring and training a floor coating crew](/blog/how-to-hire-and-train-a-floor-coating-crew) alongside your state's comp rules.

## Do floor coating contractors need pollution liability?

It is worth asking about. Coating work involves resins, solvents, cleaners, and fumes inside occupied homes and businesses. Many general liability policies contain pollution exclusions, and the exact wording decides what is covered if a homeowner claims an odor or fume problem, or a spill reaches a drain or the soil. A contractors pollution liability policy, or an endorsement, is the usual way to fill that gap. Commercial and industrial clients are more likely to require it. Ask your agent to show you the pollution exclusion in your GL and explain what it leaves out.

## When does an umbrella policy make sense?

An umbrella adds limits on top of your general liability, commercial auto, and employer's liability. It becomes worth a quote when:

- A GC or property manager asks for higher limits than your GL carries.
- You add crews, trucks, and drivers.
- You start taking commercial jobs with larger contract values.

Buying an umbrella is often a simpler way to meet a client's higher limit requirement than raising every underlying policy.

## What do homeowners and GCs ask for?

Homeowners rarely ask for more than proof you are insured and licensed where required, but the ones who ask are often the ones who check. GCs and commercial clients ask for a lot more.

| What they ask for | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Certificate of insurance (COI) | A one page summary of your policies, limits, and dates, issued by your agent | Ask your agent for a COI with the client listed as certificate holder. Never edit one yourself |
| Additional insured | The client is covered under your liability policy for claims arising from your work | Requires an endorsement on your policy. Ask whether it covers ongoing and completed operations |
| Primary and non-contributory | Your policy pays before theirs | Usually an endorsement. Confirm before you sign a contract that requires it |
| Waiver of subrogation | Your insurer gives up the right to recover from the client | Often requested on GL and workers' comp. May carry a charge |
| Specific limits | For example $2 million per occurrence, or an umbrella | Get the requirement in writing before you bid, then confirm with your agent |
| Workers' comp certificate | Proof your employees are covered | Even in states where comp is optional, some clients will not hire you without it |

Read the insurance section of every subcontract before you price the job. The insurance requirements can change your cost, and an endorsement your policy does not have can hold up payment.

## How do you keep insurance costs reasonable?

We are not going to quote premiums, because they genuinely depend on your state, your payroll, your revenue, your vehicles, your claims history, and the carrier. The way to get a real number is to get quotes from more than one licensed agent, ideally one who writes contractors. A few things tend to help:

- **Describe the work accurately** so you are classified correctly the first time.
- **Keep a clean claims record** with written prep checklists, moisture tests, and safe job sites.
- **Collect COIs from every subcontractor** so they do not end up on your audit.
- **Review coverage every year** as you add crews, equipment, and commercial work.
- **Bundle where it makes sense.** A business owner's policy or a package from one carrier can simplify things, though not every carrier offers one to contractors.

## Frequently asked questions

### What insurance does an epoxy flooring contractor need?

Most epoxy flooring contractors start with general liability that includes products and completed operations, commercial auto for business vehicles and trailers, and inland marine coverage for equipment. Workers' compensation comes next when you hire, depending on your state. Contractors pollution liability and an umbrella are common additions as you grow or take commercial work.

### How much does floor coating business insurance cost?

It varies by state, payroll, revenue, vehicles, and claims history, so any number without a quote is a guess. Get quotes from more than one licensed commercial agent, describe your work accurately, and ask each one to break the premium down by policy so you can compare.

### Does general liability cover a floor coating that peels?

Usually not the cost of redoing your own work. Liability policies are designed for injury and damage to others, and most exclude the cost to repair or replace the contractor's own faulty work. That is why a warranty reserve and good prep matter. Ask your agent how your policy treats damage your work causes to other property.

### Do I need workers' comp if I am a one person floor coating business?

It depends on your state. Many states let sole owners or LLC members exclude themselves, while employees are generally covered once you hire. California requires coverage even with one employee, while Texas lets most private employers choose. Check your state's workers' compensation agency and ask your agent.

### What is a certificate of insurance and who asks for it?

A certificate of insurance is a summary of your policies, limits, and dates, issued by your insurance agent. GCs, commercial property managers, HOAs, and some homeowners ask for one before you start work, and commercial clients often also ask to be named as additional insured on your liability policy.
