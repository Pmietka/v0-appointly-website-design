# Floor Coating Equipment List: Grinders, Dust Control, Testing, and What to Buy First

**Meta Description:** A complete floor coating equipment list by category, with typical cost ranges, the best grinder setup for garage floors, OSHA silica rules, and buy vs rent vs used.

---

A residential floor coating equipment list comes down to six categories: grinding and dust control, mixing, application, testing, PPE, and a vehicle or trailer to haul it all. The best grinder for floor coating is the one you can run with the dust collector its manufacturer recommends, because that pairing is what OSHA's silica standard for construction is built around and what keeps your prep consistent. Plan on roughly $6,000 to $15,000 for the grinder and dust extraction and $1,500 to $3,000 for everything handheld, which is where almost every new coating business should start spending.

## Key takeaways

- The grinder and its dust extractor are the most important purchase. Prep decides whether a floor lasts, and the grinder decides prep.
- Buy the dust collector as a matched pair with the grinder. OSHA's Table 1 for walk-behind floor grinders points to the dust collection system the manufacturer recommends, with a 99% or better filter and a filter-cleaning mechanism.
- Handheld edge grinders have their own Table 1 row: a shroud plus a collector moving at least 25 cfm per inch of wheel diameter, and a respirator when you run them indoors for more than four hours a shift.
- A moisture meter is cheap compared with one failed floor. Test every slab and write the reading down.
- Rent or buy used to start if cash is tight, but never skimp on dust control or respirators.
- Buy in this order: grinder and vac, edge grinder and shroud, moisture testing, PPE, mixing and application tools, then the trailer.

## What is the best grinder for floor coating?

For residential garage floors, the best grinder for floor coating is a walk-behind planetary grinder sized for two-car garages, paired with the dust extractor its manufacturer recommends, from a brand with a parts and service network near you. We are not going to name a model, because the right one depends on your power supply, your budget, and who services it locally. What matters more than the logo:

- **Planetary heads.** Multiple rotating heads give a flatter, more even scratch pattern than a single disc machine, which is what you want under a thin film coating.
- **Weight and width.** Heavier machines cut faster. Narrower machines fit garages, trailers, and one person's back. Most residential crews land in the middle.
- **Power.** Check whether the machine runs on single phase power you can find in a garage, or whether you will need a generator. This decision drives a lot of other costs.
- **Tooling availability.** If you cannot get diamonds for it overnight, it will sit in the trailer during your busiest week.
- **The matched vac.** Buy the dust collector the grinder manufacturer recommends, at or above the airflow they specify. That is also what OSHA's table expects, as the next section explains.
- **Local service.** A grinder that is down for two weeks in April costs more than the price difference between two brands.

Run a demo before you buy. Watch the scratch pattern, the dust at the skirt, and how the machine handles at a garage edge.

## What does OSHA's silica standard require for floor grinding?

Grinding concrete releases respirable crystalline silica. OSHA's [respirable crystalline silica standard for construction, 29 CFR 1926.1153](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.1153), sets a permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic meter of air as an 8 hour average, with an action level of 25. The standard applies to employers, so if you have even one employee on a grinder, it applies to you.

The practical way most small contractors comply is Table 1 of the standard, which lists common tasks and the controls that, fully and properly implemented, satisfy the rule without air monitoring. Two rows matter for coating contractors.

| Table 1 task | Engineering controls listed | Respirator, 4 hours or less per shift | Respirator, more than 4 hours per shift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (xiii) Walk-behind milling machines and floor grinders | Integrated water delivery, OR the dust collection system recommended by the manufacturer, with airflow at or above the manufacturer's recommendation and a filter of 99% or greater efficiency with a filter-cleaning mechanism. Indoors or in an enclosed area, use a HEPA-filtered vacuum to remove loose dust between passes | None | None |
| (xii) Handheld grinders for uses other than mortar removal (dust collection option) | Commercially available shroud and dust collection system, collector providing 25 cfm or more of airflow per inch of wheel diameter, 99% or greater filter efficiency, and a cyclonic pre-separator or filter-cleaning mechanism | None (outdoors or indoors) | None outdoors. APF 10 indoors or in an enclosed area |

Summarized from Table 1 of 1926.1153 as published on osha.gov. Read the full table yourself before you build your plan.

What that means on a garage job:

- **Your walk-behind grinder needs the right vac, not just any vac.** A shop vac with a paper filter does not match the Table 1 description.
- **Do the math on your edge grinder.** A 7 inch wheel at 25 cfm per inch needs a collector moving at least 175 cfm. Check the vac's rating before you buy.
- **Count the whole shift.** If one person runs more than one Table 1 task and the combined time is over four hours, the standard applies the more-than-four-hours respirator column to every task. On a long edge-work day in a garage, plan for an APF 10 respirator (a half-face respirator is the common example).
- **No dry sweeping.** The standard does not allow dry sweeping or dry brushing where it could add to exposure, unless wet sweeping or HEPA vacuuming is not feasible. HEPA vacuum the slab.
- **Paperwork.** Employers need a written exposure control plan, a designated competent person, and medical exams for any employee required to wear a respirator 30 or more days a year.

Respirators bring their own rules under OSHA's [respiratory protection standard, 29 CFR 1910.134](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134), including fit testing. OSHA's [silica in construction page](https://www.osha.gov/silica-crystalline/construction) has fact sheets and a plain language overview. This section is a summary for planning, not legal advice. Talk to OSHA's free consultation service or a safety professional if you are unsure how the rule applies to your crew.

## Do you really need a moisture meter?

Yes. Moisture vapor coming up through the slab is one of the most common reasons coatings lift, and the only way to know is to test. Our [floor coating warranty guide](/blog/floor-coating-warranty-guide) explains why your moisture exclusion only holds up if the reading is written on the estimate.

A handheld concrete moisture meter is the everyday tool. It gives a fast comparative reading so you can spot wet areas. For slabs that read high, or for basements and commercial work where the manufacturer asks for a specific test, use the method in the product data sheet. The common standards are [ASTM F2170](https://store.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) (in-situ relative humidity probes) and [ASTM F1869](https://store.astm.org/f1869-22.html) (calcium chloride test). Your coating manufacturer's limits, and the test they name, are what your warranty rides on.

## Should you buy, rent, or buy used?

Each piece of equipment has a different answer.

| Equipment | Buy new | Rent | Buy used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Walk-behind grinder | Best once you have steady weekly jobs | Good for the first few jobs or a trial run | Good value if hours, heads, and motor check out |
| Dust extractor | Best, so filters and cleaning mechanism work as designed | Rent with the grinder as a matched pair | Only with new filters and a working filter-cleaning mechanism |
| Edge grinder and shroud | Buy. Low cost, used every job | Rarely worth it | Fine for the grinder, buy the shroud new |
| Moisture meter | Buy | Not worth it | Fine if calibrated and working |
| Respirators and PPE | Always new | Never | Never |
| Shot blaster or large grinder | Only for steady commercial work | Best for occasional commercial jobs | Possible once you know the work is recurring |
| Trailer | Rarely needed new | Not practical | Most common choice |

Renting makes sense while you prove demand. Rental yards and some distributors rent grinders by the day or week, so call for local rates. Rented machines vary in condition, though, and you never learn one machine's habits.

Before buying a used grinder, run it on a slab, check the hours and the heads for wear, confirm tooling is still sold for that model, and make sure the matching vac comes with it.

## What should you buy first?

If you have a limited budget, spend it in this order:

1. **Grinder and matched dust extractor.** Rent before you buy if you have to, but do not start without both.
2. **Edge grinder, shroud, and a vac rated for it.** Garage edges are where homeowners look first.
3. **Moisture meter.** It protects your warranty and your reputation.
4. **Respirators, fit testing, and the rest of the PPE.** Non negotiable once anyone works for you.
5. **Mixing and application tools.** Cheap, but buy good squeegees and rollers. Roller marks in a topcoat are a callback.
6. **Material for three to five jobs.** Enough to say yes to the first booked estimates.
7. **The trailer.** Use the truck you have until the jobs justify it.

Then budget for wear. Diamond tooling, vac filters, roller covers, and squeegee blades are consumables. Build them into your price the way our [pricing and margins guide](/blog/floor-coating-pricing-and-margins) does, around $30 to $60 of grinding consumables per 500 square foot garage. Service the grinder and vac in the slow months, as the [winter marketing plan](/blog/winter-marketing-plan-floor-coating-contractors) suggests, so nothing breaks on the first big spring week.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does floor coating equipment cost?

For a residential garage business, plan on roughly $6,000 to $15,000 for the walk-behind grinder and dust extractor, $1,500 to $3,000 for hand tools, testing, and PPE, $2,000 to $4,000 for starting material, and $5,000 to $20,000 for a used trailer or van if you need one. Treat these as typical ranges and get quotes, because new versus used and your local supplier move them a lot.

### What is the best grinder for epoxy and polyaspartic garage floors?

A walk-behind planetary grinder sized for garages, paired with the dust collector its manufacturer recommends, from a brand you can get tooling and service for nearby. Demo it on a real slab before you buy. The pairing matters as much as the grinder, because OSHA's Table 1 for floor grinders is written around the manufacturer's recommended dust collection.

### Does OSHA's silica rule apply to small floor coating companies?

The construction silica standard, 29 CFR 1926.1153, applies to employers whose workers are exposed to respirable crystalline silica, and grinding concrete is one of the tasks it covers. If you have employees, follow Table 1 for walk-behind floor grinders and handheld grinders, keep a written exposure control plan, and avoid dry sweeping. Read the standard on osha.gov or ask OSHA's consultation service if you are unsure.

### Can you rent a floor grinder instead of buying one?

Yes. Many new contractors rent for the first few jobs, and rent larger equipment for occasional commercial work. Rent the grinder and its matched vac together. Once you coat floors every week, owning usually makes more sense.

### What testing equipment does a floor coating contractor need?

At minimum, a concrete moisture meter used on every slab, with the reading written on the estimate. For slabs that read high, or where the coating manufacturer requires it, use the test the product data sheet names, typically in-situ RH probes under ASTM F2170 or a calcium chloride test under ASTM F1869.
