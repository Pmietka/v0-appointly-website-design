# How to Hire and Train a Floor Coating Crew (Without Wrecking Your Reputation)

**Meta Description:** How floor coating contractors hire installers, what to pay, a four-week training plan, and how to know when your calendar can support a second crew.

---

## The Crew Is the Product

Homeowners do not buy polyaspartic. They buy the floor your crew leaves behind, and a coating business lives or dies on whether the second crew installs as well as the owner did. Most coating contractors hire too late, train too little, and then wonder why the callbacks start the month the new crew launches.

This is how the contractors we work with build crews that install at the owner's standard. It covers when to hire, who to hire, what to pay, and a four-week training plan that gets a new installer running floors on their own.

## When to Hire

Hire from a full calendar, not from hope. The signals that you are ready:

- The current crew is installing 14 or more floors a month for three straight months.
- Your booked estimates are scheduling out more than three weeks.
- You, the owner, are running estimates in the evening because you installed all day.

If those are true, you have the revenue to support a second installer and the demand to keep them busy. Our guide on [how many jobs one crew can do](/blog/how-many-floor-coating-jobs-can-one-crew-do) walks through the capacity math. If they are not true, the fix is more [booked estimates](/floor-coating-leads), not more payroll.

## Who to Hire

The best floor coating installers we see did not come from the coating industry. They came from trades with the same habits:

- **Painters** understand cut-in, film build, and working clean. Best first hire for most coating companies.
- **Tile and flooring installers** understand prep, layout, and working on their knees for eight hours.
- **Concrete finishers** understand slabs, moisture, and grinding, but sometimes need coaching on finish quality.
- **Detailers and auto body techs** are meticulous and used to a customer inspecting their work under bright light.

What you are hiring for is not skill with a roller. It is attention to detail, physical stamina, and the ability to show up at 7 am on a homeowner's driveway looking like someone they want in their garage.

Avoid hiring a "coating guy" from a competitor unless you have seen their floors. Bad habits in prep are harder to untrain than a blank slate.

## What to Pay

Ranges vary by market, but for a residential coating crew in most of the US:

- **Helper or apprentice:** $18 to $24 per hour
- **Lead installer:** $25 to $35 per hour, or a per-floor rate
- **Crew lead running jobs without the owner present:** $30 to $40 per hour plus a per-floor bonus

Per-floor pay works well once an installer is trained. A two-person crew paid $600 to $900 combined for a one-day garage on a $3,500 job keeps labor at roughly 20 percent of revenue, which is the number behind the margins in our [pricing and margins guide](/blog/floor-coating-pricing-and-margins). Tie any bonus to zero callbacks in 60 days, not to speed. Speed bonuses produce peeling floors.

## The Four-Week Training Plan

**Week 1: Prep only.** The new hire runs the grinder, chases cracks, and vacuums on every job. No coating. They learn to read a slab, spot old sealer, run a moisture test, and understand why the floor's life is decided before the first coat goes down. By Friday they should be able to explain the prep checklist without looking at it.

**Week 2: Base coat and flake.** They mix under supervision, back-roll behind the lead, and broadcast flake. Mixing ratios and pot life become second nature here. They also learn the cut-in at walls and around the door track, which is where homeowners look first.

**Week 3: Scrape and topcoat.** Scraping flake flat and laying a [polyaspartic](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors) topcoat without roller marks is the skill that separates a professional floor from a driveway-special. The lead watches every pass this week.

**Week 4: Run a floor.** The new installer runs a job with the lead present but silent. The owner inspects under work lights before the homeowner does. Pass that inspection twice and they are ready to be the second person on a crew. Pass it five times and they can lead one.

Put the checklist for every stage on paper and in the truck. A written checklist is the only way two crews produce the same floor.

## Teach the Customer Side Too

An installer who coats a perfect floor and tracks mud through the homeowner's kitchen still costs you a review. Train these on day one:

- Text the homeowner the morning of the job with an arrival window.
- Walk the garage with the homeowner before starting and confirm the color and the cure-time instructions.
- Protect the driveway, the door frames, and anything the homeowner could not move.
- Do a final walkthrough with the homeowner, hand them the care sheet and the warranty, and ask for a [Google review](/blog/google-reviews-floor-coating-contractors) while they are standing on the new floor.

The walkthrough and the review ask should be part of the job, not an afterthought for the owner to handle later.

## Keeping Good Installers

Coating crews quit for two reasons: no work in the slow months and no path forward. Fix both.

For the slow months, plan the [seasonal calendar](/blog/best-time-of-year-for-floor-coating-jobs) in advance. Basements, commercial floors, and shop floors keep a crew busy in winter, and a full calendar in spring depends on marketing that started in February.

For the path forward, publish it. Helper to installer at 90 days if they pass inspections. Installer to crew lead when they run 20 floors with no callbacks. Crew lead gets a per-floor bonus and a say in hiring the next helper. People stay where they can see the next step.

## The Second Crew Needs Its Own Demand

The most common mistake is hiring the second crew and splitting the same 25 estimates a month between two teams. Both crews end up half-busy and the new one leaves. A second crew needs its own flow of estimates, which usually means widening the service area or adding a second [exclusive territory](/exclusive-floor-coating-leads). Plan the estimate volume before the first paycheck.

## Build the Crew, Then Keep It Busy

Hire from a full calendar, pay for quality instead of speed, train for four weeks on a written checklist, and give people a reason to stay.
