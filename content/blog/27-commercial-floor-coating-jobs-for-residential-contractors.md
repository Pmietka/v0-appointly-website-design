# Commercial Floor Coating Jobs: How Residential Contractors Land Them, and When They Shouldn't

**Meta Description:** How residential floor coating contractors land commercial concrete coating work, what it pays, what it costs to bid, and when to stay focused on garages.

---

## The Big-Job Temptation

Every garage floor contractor eventually gets the call: a warehouse, a brewery, a car dealership, or a school kitchen wants 8,000 square feet coated. The number on that quote is larger than a month of garages, and it is easy to reorganize the whole business around chasing it. Sometimes that is right. Often it is a trap.

This guide lays out what commercial floor coating work actually pays, what it costs to win, what changes on the job, and a simple rule for deciding whether it belongs in your business.

## What Commercial Coating Work Pays

Residential garages sell on transformation and price out at $6 to $9 per square foot for a full flake system. Commercial work sells on specification and durability, and the per-foot number is usually lower while the total is higher:

- **Warehouse and light industrial floors** (thin-mil epoxy or urethane): $2 to $5 per square foot
- **Showrooms, retail, and office** (decorative flake or metallic): $5 to $9 per square foot
- **Commercial kitchens, breweries, and food processing** (urethane cement, cove base, chemical resistance): $8 to $15 per square foot
- **Aircraft hangars and dealerships** (high-build, high-gloss systems): $4 to $8 per square foot

A 10,000 square foot warehouse at $3.50 is a $35,000 job. That is ten garages of revenue in one invoice, which is the appeal. Gross margin on straightforward commercial work usually lands at 30 to 45 percent, well below the 50 to 60 percent a good residential crew keeps on garages. Bigger check, thinner margin.

## What Changes on a Commercial Job

**Bidding.** Residential work is an estimate at the kitchen table and a decision the same week. Commercial work is a written proposal against a spec, often competing with three to six bidders, with a decision weeks or months later. Expect to win one in four or five bids you spend real time on.

**Paperwork.** Certificates of insurance with higher limits, sometimes $2 million or more. W-9s, lien waivers, safety plans, and occasionally prevailing wage on public work. A general contractor may hold 10 percent retainage for months.

**Schedule.** Nights and weekends are normal because the facility cannot shut down. Your crew coats a grocery store at 2 am.

**Equipment.** A 10,000 square foot floor needs a larger grinder, more dust extraction, and enough manpower to finish a pour before it kicks. Renting is fine for the first few jobs; buying is a $15,000 to $40,000 decision.

**Cash flow.** Net 30 or net 60 terms mean you front materials and labor for a $35,000 job and wait. One slow-paying general contractor can starve a small coating company that is used to a check on the day of install.

**Prep and product.** Moisture mitigation, joint filling, cove bases, and slip-resistant broadcast are common line items. A crew trained only on garage flake systems has real gaps here. Our guide to [hiring and training a crew](/blog/how-to-hire-and-train-a-floor-coating-crew) covers the fundamentals, but commercial work usually needs manufacturer training on the specific system.

## The Honest Case for Commercial

Commercial coating work solves the two problems that keep residential contractors up at night:

1. **Seasonality.** Warehouses do not care that it is February. Commercial jobs fill the [winter gap](/blog/best-time-of-year-for-floor-coating-jobs) when garage demand drops.
2. **Crew utilization.** A three-night commercial job keeps a second crew paid during a slow week.

It also builds a portfolio that opens doors: a coated dealership floor gets you the next dealership.

## The Honest Case Against It

- **Bid time is unpaid.** Twenty hours on a proposal you lose four times out of five is a real cost.
- **Margin compression.** You are bidding against specialists with bigger equipment and lower per-foot costs.
- **Cash risk.** Fronting $12,000 in material for a client that pays in 60 days can sink a company built on same-day checks.
- **Focus.** Every hour on commercial bids is an hour not spent on the residential system that pays 55 percent margins. Some of the most profitable coating businesses we work with never coat a single warehouse.

## A Simple Rule

Take commercial work when all three are true:

1. **The job came to you**, through a referral or a past customer, rather than a public bid list.
2. **You can be paid on completion or with a deposit** covering materials.
3. **It fills a slot your residential calendar could not fill anyway**, such as a winter week or a night shift.

If any of those fail, quote it high enough that winning is a happy accident, and get back to garages.

## How to Land Commercial Work Without Chasing It

The best commercial jobs come from the residential work you already do. The homeowner whose garage you coated owns a machine shop. The realtor who referred you manages a strip mall. Ask every residential customer, at the walkthrough, one question: "Do you know anyone with a commercial floor that needs help?"

Beyond that:

- **Photograph every garage like a showroom.** Commercial buyers look at your [Google profile](/blog/google-reviews-floor-coating-contractors) too, and a page of glossy floors sells better than a spec sheet.
- **Introduce yourself to two or three local general contractors and property managers.** Bring a sample board and a one-page capabilities sheet. You want to be the coating sub they call, not a name on a bid list.
- **Coat your own shop or a friend's** in a commercial-grade system so you have one real commercial photo set.
- **Say yes to small commercial first.** A 1,500 square foot auto shop teaches you the paperwork and the night schedule on a job that cannot hurt you.
