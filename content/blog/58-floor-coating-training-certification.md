# Floor Coating Training and Epoxy Flooring Certification: What It Is Worth and Where to Get It

**Meta Description:** Where to get floor coating training, what an epoxy flooring certification does and does not mean, how licensing differs by state, and what to practice first.

---

Floor coating training comes from four places: manufacturer and distributor classes, franchise training, independent hands-on courses, and working on an established crew. An epoxy flooring certification usually means you finished a specific company's class on its products. It is not a state license, it is not a legal requirement in most places, and it does not replace the floors you install under supervision before you charge a homeowner.

## Key takeaways

- Most "certifications" in floor coating are issued by a manufacturer, distributor, or training company for its own class or system. Treat them as proof of training, not proof of skill.
- A contractor license is a separate, state specific question. Some states license specialty trades at the state level, some leave it to cities and counties.
- The best training is a short hands-on class followed by real floors under an experienced installer.
- Practice prep before anything else: grinding, edges, crack repair, and moisture testing.
- Coat at least three floors you are not paid full price for before you sell your first one.
- OSHA's Outreach 10 and 30 hour classes are useful safety training, but OSHA says plainly that none of them is a certification.

## What kinds of floor coating training are there?

Each route trades cost, time, and depth differently.

| Route | What you get | Typical cost and time | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manufacturer or distributor class | Hands-on time with their products, prep, and system install steps | Usually a few days, often a modest fee, sometimes tied to buying their system | Training is built around one product line |
| Franchise training | Classroom and field training on prep, install, sales, and software | Bundled into the franchise fee | Only available if you buy the franchise |
| Independent hands-on course | Broader product exposure, sometimes business and estimating topics | A few days to a week | Quality varies a lot. Ask how much time is spent actually grinding and coating |
| Working on an established crew | Hundreds of floors, real problems, real homeowners | Paid, months not days | Slower, and you may learn the owner's bad habits too |

### Manufacturer and distributor classes

Many coating manufacturers and distributors run their own classes, because installers who know the system produce fewer warranty claims. Two examples we verified on the companies' own sites at the time of writing:

- **Simiron** lists [Simiron University training classes](https://simiron.com/trainings/concrete-coating-training/) as two-day, hands-on sessions with its technical team, covering decorative chip and one-day flake systems, metallics, concrete coatings, and concrete prep and moisture testing. Its page lists the class at $500 per person, with dates in several cities.
- **Xtreme Polishing Systems** lists [epoxy coating classes](https://xtremepolishingsystems.com/pages/epoxy-coatings-classes) that it describes as a five-day, hands-on course covering moisture testing, moisture barriers, repair and leveling, primers and base coats, tools and equipment, and estimating. The page states that the classes are sold and conducted by Polished Concrete University, and lists pricing by class.

Schedules and prices change, so confirm details with each company directly. We do not have a relationship with either one, and these are examples, not endorsements. If you already buy from a distributor, ask whether they run training days for the systems they sell. Many do, and some are low cost for customers.

### Franchise training

A franchise gives you training as part of the package, usually a mix of classroom time and field work on its own system, plus sales and software training. The tradeoff is the upfront fee and ongoing royalty. Our breakdown of [garage coating franchise vs independent](/blog/garage-coating-franchise-vs-independent) covers what to read in the disclosure document before you sign.

### Working for an established crew

The slowest route is often the best one. Six months on a busy crew puts you on more floors than any class can, including the ugly ones: the slab with old sealer, the garage that tested wet, the homeowner who drove on it a day early. If you can get hired by a good installer and learn on their dime, take it.

## What does an epoxy flooring certification actually mean?

It means you completed a specific company's training on its products. That has real value, but it is narrower than the word suggests.

**What certification can do:**

- Show a homeowner or commercial client that you were trained on the system you are installing.
- Give you access to technical support, and in some cases a manufacturer's material warranty or installer program. Ask each manufacturer exactly what their program includes, because terms vary.
- Get you listed on a manufacturer's or distributor's installer locator, if they run one.

**What certification does not do:**

- It is not a contractor license. A class certificate does not let you contract in a state that requires a license.
- It is not an industry wide standard. There is no single national epoxy flooring certification that every homeowner or state recognizes.
- It does not make you good. A two-day class is enough to understand the steps. It is not enough to read a slab you have never seen before.

The same goes for safety training. OSHA's [Outreach Training Program](https://www.osha.gov/training/outreach) offers 10 hour and 30 hour construction classes through authorized trainers, and they are worth taking if you run a crew. But OSHA's own page states that none of the Outreach courses is considered a certification, and that the program does not fulfill an employer's training requirements under specific OSHA standards like the silica rule. Our [floor coating equipment list](/blog/floor-coating-equipment-list) covers what that silica rule asks for on a grinder.

## Do you need a license to install floor coatings?

It depends on the state, and sometimes on the city and the job size. Contractor licensing is state specific, and coating work does not always fit neatly into one classification. Two examples show how different the systems are:

- **California.** The [Contractors State License Board (CSLB)](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/) licenses contractors at the state level and publishes a list of [specialty classifications](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/About_Us/Library/Licensing_Classifications/), including C-15 Flooring and Floor Covering and C-33 Painting and Decorating. Which one fits your work is a question to put to the CSLB, not to a training company.
- **Florida.** The [Construction Industry Licensing Board](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/construction-industry/) separates certified contractors, who can contract anywhere in the state, from registered contractors, who meet local competency requirements and can contract only in those jurisdictions.

In other states, coatings may fall under a home improvement registration, a general contractor license above a job value threshold, or only a local business license. Before your first paid job:

1. Search your state's contractor licensing board and read how it treats flooring and coating work.
2. Call the board if the classifications are unclear. Describe the work in plain terms: grinding concrete and applying epoxy and polyaspartic coatings in residential garages.
3. Check your city and county for business licenses and any local registration.
4. Get your LLC, insurance, and a written contract in place. The startup steps are in [how to start a floor coating business](/blog/how-to-start-a-floor-coating-business).

## What should you practice before your first paid job?

Prep, then cut-in, then topcoat. In that order, because prep is where floors fail and topcoat is where homeowners look.

| Skill | How to practice | You are ready when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grinding | Grind your own garage and a few sample slabs. Change tooling, watch the scratch pattern | The profile is even edge to edge with no swirl marks or missed spots |
| Edges and corners | Run the edge grinder along walls, under the door track, around drains | The edges match the field of the floor |
| Crack and spall repair | Chase and fill cracks on a scrap slab | Repairs are flush and invisible after flake |
| Moisture testing | Test every slab you touch, including your own | You know the reading and what it means for the product you use |
| Mixing | Mix small batches with a timer and a scale | Ratios and pot life are automatic, no guessing |
| Base coat and flake | Lay base and broadcast on sample boards and a practice floor | Even coverage with no bare spots or puddles |
| Scrape and topcoat | Scrape flake and roll topcoat on a full practice floor | No roller marks, even sheen, clean cut-in at the walls |

A practical plan:

1. **Take one hands-on class** on the system you plan to sell, ideally a manufacturer or distributor class so you have support afterward.
2. **Coat three floors at cost:** your own garage, a family member's, and a friend's. Those three floors become your first portfolio photos.
3. **Install at least three floors under an experienced installer,** paying for their time if you have to.
4. **Write a prep checklist** and follow it on every job. The four-week plan in our guide to [hiring and training a floor coating crew](/blog/how-to-hire-and-train-a-floor-coating-crew) works for a new owner as well as a new hire.
5. **Write your warranty** around what you were trained to do. Our [floor coating warranty guide](/blog/floor-coating-warranty-guide) explains why it should only cover slabs you ground and moisture tested.

Stick to one system at launch. A full flake floor with a [polyaspartic topcoat](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors) is what most residential crews start with. Metallics, quartz, and commercial systems can wait until you have done fifty garages.

## How should you talk about training with homeowners?

Plainly. Homeowners care less about a certificate than about whether you will show up, grind the floor, and stand behind it. Mention the training once, then explain your prep, your moisture test, and your warranty. If you are certified on the system you sell, say so and say what it means: "We are trained on this manufacturer's system, and that is the system your warranty covers." Do not imply a license or a government credential you do not have.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you need a certification to do epoxy flooring?

Usually not. Most states do not require an epoxy flooring certification. They may require a contractor license, which is a separate thing issued by a state or local board. Manufacturer certifications can help with product support, warranties, and credibility, so they are worth having, but they are not a legal requirement in most places.

### How long does floor coating training take?

Manufacturer and distributor classes typically run a few days to a week. That is enough to learn the steps for one system. Becoming consistent takes real floors, which is why we recommend at least three supervised installs and three floors at cost before your first full price job.

### How much does epoxy flooring training cost?

It varies by provider and length. As examples, Simiron's site lists its two-day class at $500 per person, and Xtreme Polishing Systems lists pricing for its five-day epoxy class on its page. Some distributors run low cost training days for customers. Confirm current pricing directly with any provider.

### Is a manufacturer certification the same as a contractor license?

No. A manufacturer certification shows you completed training on that company's products. A contractor license is issued by a state or local licensing authority and may be required to legally contract for the work. Check your state's licensing board, such as the CSLB in California or the Construction Industry Licensing Board in Florida.

### What should a new floor coating installer practice first?

Grinding and edge work, then crack repair and moisture testing. Prep decides whether a floor lasts, so practice it until the profile is even edge to edge. After that, practice mixing, base coat and flake, and topcoat on sample boards and practice floors.
