# The Three Objections Every Floor Coating Estimator Hears, and How to Answer Them

**Meta Description:** The three objections every floor coating estimator hears, cheaper one-day guys, talk to my spouse, just getting quotes, and answers that hold up in a garage.

---

Every floor coating estimator hears the same three objections: "the one day guys are cheaper," "I need to talk to my spouse," and "I am just getting quotes." There is no single close rate benchmark, because close rate depends on the product and the estimator's sales skill. But coached estimators close meaningfully better, and the reason is simple: the objections repeat, so the answers can be practiced.

## Key takeaways

- The three objections that repeat on floor coating estimates are price against one-day installers, needing to talk to a spouse, and just collecting quotes.
- Answer the price objection with what the cheaper quote leaves out: moisture testing, diamond grinding instead of acid etching, a real topcoat, and a warranty that will still be honored in year three.
- Prevent the spouse objection at booking by asking who else needs to be there, and when only one person is home, book a second short visit instead of leaving a quote behind.
- Reframe "just getting quotes" by handing the homeowner a five-item checklist, because most competing quotes are for different systems.
- Most objections are created by the estimator: no good, better, best options, no price anchor before the number, and quotes sent by email instead of presented in the garage.

## Why is there no single close rate benchmark for floor coating estimates?

There is no single close rate benchmark because close rate depends on two things that vary from one contractor to the next: the product being sold and the skill of the person selling it. A full polyaspartic system at the top of the market and an epoxy base with a polyaspartic topcoat in a saturated Florida market are different conversations at different prices. Our [floor coating benchmarks](/floor-coating-benchmarks) page publishes lead to appointment rate and show rate but not close rate, for that reason.

What we can say is that coached estimators close meaningfully better than uncoached ones, because the objections are the same three in nearly every market, on nearly every driveway. The difference between a strong closer and a weak one is mostly preparation. The full estimate process is in [how to close more floor coating estimates](/blog/how-to-close-more-floor-coating-estimates); this post is only about the three moments where the homeowner pushes back.

## "The one day guys are cheaper"

The answer to "the one day guys are cheaper" is to agree that they are, then walk the homeowner through exactly what the lower price leaves out. Never argue about the number. Argue about the floor.

Start with prep, because that is where the cheap quote saves its money. A one-day price usually means acid etching instead of diamond grinding. Etching opens the surface a little; grinding removes the weak top layer of the slab and gives the coating something to bite into. Say it plainly: "The reason they can do it in a day and do it cheaper is that they skip the grind. Grinding is most of my labor, and it's the reason my floors don't peel."

Then moisture testing. Ask whether the other quote included a moisture test on the slab. Most did not. Moisture coming up through a garage slab will lift any coating from underneath, and the only way to know is to test before you coat. If the homeowner does not know whether anyone tested, that is your point made.

Then the topcoat. Many cheap quotes are a single coat of epoxy or a thin flake system with no real wear layer. Explain what a polyaspartic topcoat does for UV yellowing, hot tire pickup, and chemical resistance, and let the homeowner hold a sample of each. The system differences are in the [polyaspartic versus epoxy](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors) post; on the driveway, keep it to two sentences and a sample.

Then the warranty. Ask what the cheaper warranty covers, for how long, and whether the company will still be around to honor it. A warranty that excludes moisture and delamination covers almost nothing that actually goes wrong.

Finish with what a failed floor costs. Use your own numbers, but the shape is this: "Say the cheap floor is $2,500 and mine is $4,000. If theirs peels in two years, someone has to grind the failed coating off before the slab can be recoated. That removal is real labor, and then you're paying for the floor again. The cheap floor is the expensive one if it fails once." That is an illustrative comparison; plug in what you charge.

## "I need to talk to my spouse"

The answer to "I need to talk to my spouse" is to make sure you rarely hear it, by getting both decision makers on the estimate when it is booked. Whoever books your estimates should ask, every time, "Is there anyone else who should be there for the decision, like a spouse?" and then pick a time when both are home. The wording for that question is in the [follow-up scripts post](/blog/floor-coating-lead-follow-up-scripts).

When only one person is home anyway, the goal shifts from closing to setting up the close. Do not leave a paper quote and hope. Try this, in order:

1. **Ask if the spouse can join by phone.** "Would it help to get [Name] on speaker for five minutes so I can answer their questions directly?" A surprising number say yes.
2. **Give the present spouse the story, not just the number.** Walk them through the prep, the system, and the warranty, so they can explain why the price is what it is. A number without the story loses to the cheaper number every time.
3. **Book the next step before you leave.** "Let me come back Thursday at 6 for 15 minutes when you're both here, and I'll bring the samples again." A vague "I'll follow up" is the same as losing the job.
4. **Send the quote with the samples in it.** If you must send something, send photos of the flake colors they liked next to their garage, plus the warranty, plus the price. Make the absent spouse see the floor, not a line item.

The estimator's job when one spouse is missing is to leave with a scheduled second conversation. Judge yourself on that, not on whether you got a signature.

## "I am just getting quotes"

The answer to "I am just getting quotes" is to agree that they should, then change what they compare. Most homeowners collecting three quotes are comparing three prices for three different floors, and they do not know it.

Say something like: "Good, you should. Can I give you five things to check on every quote, including mine? Most of the quotes I see in this area are for different systems, so the prices don't line up." Then hand them the checklist, on paper or as a text:

- **Prep method:** diamond grinding or acid etching, and does the quote say which?
- **Moisture test:** was one done, and what was the reading?
- **System and thickness:** what base coat, what topcoat, and how many mils total?
- **Warranty:** how many years, what it excludes, and whether it covers peeling and moisture.
- **Crew:** who is installing the floor, the company's own crew or a subcontractor?

This makes you the contractor who told the truth about the market, and it makes every other quote defend itself against your standard. When the homeowner calls the cheaper contractor and asks what the moisture reading was, the silence on the other end does your selling.

Then ask for the decision anyway. "If the other quotes come in on the same system and I'm not the best value, I'd rather you go with them. If they don't, can I count on the job?" Most homeowners will tell you honestly where you stand.

## What mistakes create objections in the first place?

Most objections are created before the homeowner ever says them, by three mistakes in how the estimate is run. Fix these and you will hear the three objections less often.

**No good, better, best.** One price invites a yes or no. Three options, a basic epoxy with polyaspartic topcoat, a thicker flake system, and a full polyaspartic or polyurea system, invite a choice. The homeowner who was going to say "the other guys are cheaper" now has a cheaper option from you, and most pick the middle.

**No price anchoring.** If the first number the homeowner hears is your final number, every other number sounds better. Anchor before you quote: mention what a full polyaspartic system runs at the top of the market, mention what a failed floor costs to redo, then present your options. The middle option should feel like relief, not a stretch.

**Quoting by email.** A quote sent by email is a number with no story, read next to two other numbers, by a homeowner who cannot ask a question. Present the price in the garage, with the samples on the slab, while both decision makers are standing there. If you cannot, book the return visit before you leave.

## How to practice objection answers with a crew

Practice objection answers the way crews practice anything else: short, repeated, and out loud. Ten minutes at the start of a Monday meeting is enough.

1. Write each objection on a card, with the answer underneath in the estimator's own words. Not a script to read; a shape to follow.
2. Pair off. One person plays the homeowner and delivers the objection with attitude. The other answers. Swap after two rounds.
3. Record one real estimate a week, with the homeowner's permission, and listen to the objection moment together. Most estimators are surprised by how fast they drop to a discount.
4. Track which objection killed each lost job. If "talk to my spouse" leads the list, the fix is at booking, not on the driveway. If price leads, the fix is anchoring and options.

An estimator who has answered "the one day guys are cheaper" 30 times in practice does not flinch when it comes up for real. That calm is most of what "sales skill" means in this trade.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most common objections on a floor coating estimate?

The three objections that repeat on floor coating estimates are "the one day guys are cheaper," "I need to talk to my spouse," and "I am just getting quotes." Nearly every estimator in every market hears all three. Because they repeat, each one can be answered with a prepared explanation of prep, moisture testing, topcoat, warranty, and what to compare between quotes.

### How do I compete with cheaper one-day epoxy installers?

Compete with cheaper one-day installers by explaining what their price leaves out rather than matching it. Ask whether their quote includes diamond grinding, a moisture test, a polyaspartic topcoat, and a warranty that covers peeling. Then show what a failed floor costs to grind off and redo. Homeowners who understand the difference often choose the better floor.

### Should I give the homeowner a price if the spouse is not home?

Give the price, but give it with the full explanation of prep, system, and warranty, and book a second conversation before you leave. A number handed to one spouse to relay to another loses to the cheaper number. A scheduled 15-minute return visit or a phone call with both decision makers is what turns that estimate into a job.

### Should I offer a discount to close a floor coating estimate on the spot?

A discount should be the last tool, not the first. Most estimators drop price at the first objection, which teaches the homeowner that the first number was padded. Answer the objection with prep, warranty, and a good, better, best choice first. If you do offer something, tie it to a decision today and keep it small.
