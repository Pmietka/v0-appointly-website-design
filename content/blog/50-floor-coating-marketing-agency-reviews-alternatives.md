# Floor Coating Marketing Agency Reviews: How to Vet an Agency, and the Alternatives Worth Comparing

## Key takeaways

- An agency's own testimonials are curated. Google reviews, BBB complaints, and a phone call with a real client in a similar market tell you more.
- A useful review names the reviewer's company and describes an outcome. "Great team, very responsive" tells you nothing about booked estimates.
- A real case study shows ad spend, leads, booked estimates, close rate, average job, and the market. Percentages with no base are marketing.
- Ask who owns the ad account and what you keep if you leave. Either answer can be fine, but you need to know it before signing.
- Compare alternatives by model: retainer agencies bill for the work, marketplaces bill per lead, and pay per appointment providers bill per booked estimate.

## Why are floor coating agency reviews hard to read?

Testimonials on an agency's website are chosen by the agency, so they are a highlight reel. Many reviews describe the relationship (friendly account manager, quick replies) rather than estimates on a calendar. And many agencies serve several trades, so a five star review from a roofer says little about coating campaigns. Read reviews for evidence, not for stars.

## How do you read an agency's Google reviews?

Open the agency's Google Business Profile and read the reviews themselves, not the average.

- **Look for named coating companies.** A reviewer whose profile or text identifies a floor or concrete coating business is far more relevant than an anonymous one.
- **Look for specifics.** "Booked 14 estimates in our first month" or "close rate went up after they started qualifying calls" is evidence. "Amazing" is not.
- **Check the spread of dates.** A steady flow over years reads differently than a burst of reviews in one week.
- **Read the negative reviews and the replies.** How an agency answers a complaint tells you how it will answer yours.

Two rules from the platforms are worth knowing. Google's [Maps content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) treats paid or incentivized reviews and reviews from conflicts of interest (such as employees or business relationships) as fake engagement, and prohibits selectively asking only happy customers. The FTC's [final rule on fake reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) bans fake reviews, buying reviews conditioned on positive sentiment, and undisclosed insider reviews. If an agency offers to "boost" your reviews in ways that break those rules, it may be doing the same for itself.

## What does a BBB profile tell you?

A BBB profile is a complaint and transparency check, not a performance score. According to BBB's [overview of its ratings](https://www.bbb.org/overview-of-bbb-ratings), the letter grade reflects factors like complaint history, time in business, transparency, licensing, and advertising issues, and customer reviews are not part of the grade. BBB also says an "NR" (no rating) is not a negative grade.

Look for complaint themes (one complaint is noise, several about billing after cancellation is a pattern), whether complaints get answered, and whether the legal name matches the contract you are sent. Many good agencies have thin BBB profiles, so a missing profile is not a red flag on its own. Unanswered complaints are.

## How do you ask for references in your market?

Ask for two or three current clients in markets of similar size and saturation to yours, because a small town result says little about a Florida metro. Then call them. Fifteen minutes each is enough. Ask:

1. How long have you worked with them, and what do you pay for (retainer, per lead, per appointment)?
2. In a normal month, how many leads and how many booked estimates do you get?
3. Who calls the leads, and how fast?
4. What percentage of the leads were real homeowners in your area who wanted coating work?
5. What happened in a slow month? Did anything change on the invoice?
6. If you left tomorrow, what would you keep?

If the agency will not give references, or the only reference is in a different trade, treat that as information. ## What does a real case study include?

A real case study lets you rebuild the math yourself. A marketing case study gives you a big percentage and a logo.

| Should include | Why it matters | Warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market and service area | Results in a small town do not transfer to a saturated metro | "A client in the Southeast" |
| Monthly ad spend | Lets you judge cost per estimate | Spend left out entirely |
| Leads and booked estimates | Separates lead volume from calendar volume | Only "leads generated" |
| Close rate and how it is counted | Shows whether appointments were real buyers | "300% growth" with no base number |
| Average job size | Turns appointments into revenue you can verify | Revenue claims with no job count |
| The owner, named | You can call or look them up | Anonymous quotes |

## Why does ad account ownership matter?

Because it decides what you keep if you part ways. There are two common setups:

- **Campaigns in your ad account.** You own the history, the pixel data, and the audiences. Google lets you [grant access to your Google Ads account](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6372672) at different [access levels](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9978556), and Meta lets a business [add an ad account to its business portfolio](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/910137316041095) and share access with partners. If an agency runs your campaigns, ask for admin access in writing.
- **Campaigns in the provider's ad account.** Some providers, including pay per appointment models like ours, run ads from their own account. That is normal when you are buying appointments rather than marketing services, but you will not take those campaigns with you, so make sure what you are paying for (the estimates) is countable and in your hands.

Neither setup is wrong. Not knowing which one you have is.

## What are the red flags when vetting an agency?

- **No clear answer to "what do I pay if no estimates book?"** That tells you who carries the risk.
- **Reporting built on impressions, clicks, and "leads"** with no booked estimates or closed jobs.
- **Long contracts or notice periods** buried deep in the agreement.
- **"Exclusive" without a number.** Ask how many coating contractors they serve within your service radius.
- **Nobody calls the lead.** If their job ends when the form is submitted, speed to lead is your problem.
- **Guarantees with no definition.** A "guarantee" is only as good as the written terms behind it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check reviews for a floor coating marketing agency?

Read the agency's Google reviews for named coating contractors and specific outcomes, check its BBB profile for complaint patterns and responses, and call two or three current clients in markets like yours. Treat the testimonials on the agency's own website as a starting point, not proof.

### What should a floor coating marketing case study include?

The market, monthly ad spend, leads, booked estimates, close rate and how it was counted, average job size, and a named owner. With those numbers you can work out cost per estimate and cost per installed floor yourself. Without them, a case study is just a claim.

### Should I own the ad account when I hire a marketing agency?

If you are paying an agency to run your marketing, owning the ad account (with the agency given access) means you keep the history and data if you leave. If you are buying booked appointments, the provider usually runs ads from its own account, which is fine as long as the appointments you pay for are clearly defined and countable.

### What are the alternatives to a floor coating marketing agency?

There are three main models: retainer and service-fee agencies that bill for marketing work, shared lead marketplaces that bill per lead, and pay per appointment providers that bill per booked estimate. You can also run your own Meta ads if you have someone to call every lead fast.
