# How to Start a Floor Coating Business: Costs, Equipment, and Your First 20 Estimates

**Meta Description:** A practical guide to starting a garage floor coating business: startup costs, equipment, pricing, licensing, and how to book your first 20 estimates.

---

## Why Floor Coating Is One of the Best Trades to Start Right Now

A two-person crew can install a full flake garage floor in one day, charge $2,500 to $5,000 for it, and keep 50 to 60 percent of that as gross profit. The equipment fits in a trailer, the training takes weeks rather than years, and homeowners can see the result in a single before-and-after photo. That combination is why so many contractors from adjacent trades are adding coatings, and why so many new owner-operators are starting with them.

This guide covers what it actually takes to start a floor coating business: the money, the gear, the skills, the paperwork, and the part most guides skip, which is how you get the first 20 estimates on the calendar.

## Step 1: Decide What You Are Selling

Most residential coating businesses sell one core product: a full broadcast flake system with a [polyaspartic](/blog/polyaspartic-vs-epoxy-garage-floors) topcoat for garages. It installs in a day, resists hot tire pickup, and photographs beautifully. Add-ons like basements, patios, and small commercial floors come later.

Resist the urge to offer six systems on day one. One system, installed perfectly and priced with confidence, will outsell a menu.

## Step 2: Startup Costs

Realistic ranges for a residential-focused startup:

- **Grinder and dust extraction:** $6,000 to $15,000. A quality planetary grinder with a HEPA vac is the single most important purchase. Rent one for your first few jobs if cash is tight, but plan to own it.
- **Hand tools and prep gear:** $1,500 to $3,000. Edge grinder, crack chasing tools, squeegees, spiked shoes, rollers, mixing drills, moisture meter, and PPE.
- **Trailer or van:** $5,000 to $20,000 used. You need enclosed, lockable storage that keeps material out of temperature swings.
- **Initial material:** $2,000 to $4,000. Enough base coat, flake, and topcoat for your first three to five jobs.
- **Insurance, LLC, and licensing:** $1,500 to $4,000 in year one, depending on the state.
- **Training:** $0 to $3,000. Manufacturer training days are often free or cheap if you buy their system.
- **Marketing to get the first jobs:** $2,000 to $5,000. More on this below.

Add it up and most people start a coating business for $20,000 to $45,000, with the low end assuming used equipment and a vehicle you already own.

## Step 3: Learn to Prep Like a Professional

Coatings fail because of prep, not product. Every warranty claim you will ever handle traces back to moisture, contamination, or a slab that was not ground to the right profile. Before you sell a single floor:

- Install at least three floors under someone who has done hundreds. Pay for the training if you have to.
- Learn to read a slab: moisture testing, spotting sealers and old coatings, identifying spalling that needs repair.
- Build a written prep checklist and follow it on every job, no matter how simple the floor looks.

Your first year's reputation is built entirely on floors that still look perfect two summers later.

## Step 4: Price for Profit From Day One

New coating contractors almost always underprice, usually because they compare themselves to the DIY kit at the hardware store instead of to the other professionals in town. Use these ranges as a starting point:

- **Basic epoxy:** $4 to $6 per square foot
- **Full flake polyaspartic system:** $6 to $9 per square foot
- **Premium systems and decorative finishes:** $7 to $12 per square foot

A 500 square foot two-car garage at $7 per square foot is a $3,500 job. Material on that job runs $700 to $1,000, labor for two people for a day is $600 to $900, and consumables are around $150. That leaves roughly $1,750 to $2,050 in gross profit. The full breakdown is in our guide to [floor coating pricing and margins](/blog/floor-coating-pricing-and-margins).

Charge for the transformation, not the material. Homeowners are buying the best-looking room in the house.

## Step 5: Handle the Paperwork

- **Form an LLC** and open a separate business bank account before you take a deposit.
- **General liability insurance** of at least $1 million is standard, and some homeowners' associations and commercial clients will ask for a certificate.
- **Licensing** varies. Some states require a specialty contractor license for coatings, some fold it into general contracting, and some require nothing beyond a business license. Check with your state contractor board before your first job.
- **Contracts and warranties:** Use a written agreement with scope, price, deposit, and a clear warranty that excludes moisture failures on slabs that fail a moisture test.

## Step 6: Build Proof Before You Need It

You will not have reviews on day one, so make proof out of the work itself. Photograph every floor: before, during grind, flake broadcast, and finished. Shoot a short phone video of the reveal. Ask every customer for a Google review while you are still standing in their garage, ideally with an [NFC review card](/blog/nfc-review-cards-floor-coating-contractors). Five reviews with photos will outperform a beautiful website with none.

Coat your own garage, a family member's garage, and a friend's garage at cost in the first month. Those three floors are your portfolio.

## Step 7: Get Your First 20 Estimates

This is where most new coating businesses stall. The floors are good, the pricing is right, and the calendar is empty. Here is the order that works:

**Weeks 1 to 2: Free channels.** Set up your [Google Business Profile](/blog/google-reviews-floor-coating-contractors) with photos, post your first three floors in local Facebook groups, and tell every contractor you know that you now do coatings. Expect two to five estimates from this.

**Weeks 2 to 6: Meta ads.** Facebook and Instagram ads with a real before-and-after of a local garage are the fastest way to create demand for a new coating business. ## Step 8: Plan Around the Season

Demand for garage floors peaks in spring and early summer, dips in the heat, and drops in winter everywhere the slab gets cold. Polyaspartic widens the winter window because it cures at lower temperatures, but you should still expect your calendar to swing. Start marketing in late winter so you enter [peak season](/blog/best-time-of-year-for-floor-coating-jobs) with a backlog, and use slow months for basements and commercial work.

## What a Realistic First Year Looks Like

A one-crew coating business that runs two to three garage floors a week at an average of $3,500 produces $28,000 to $42,000 a month in revenue during season, with gross profit around 50 to 60 percent before marketing and overhead. Most first-year owner-operators land well below that while they learn, then hit it in year two once the calendar is predictable.

The single biggest variable is not skill. It is whether you have a system that keeps estimates coming while you are on the job. Solve that early and the rest of the business is grinding, flaking, and cashing checks.
